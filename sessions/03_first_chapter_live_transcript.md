## Shared Context (All Agents Read This)

### Session 0 Decision: Definition

A Handbook for Living is:

> A clearing — the space a man returns to when every teaching he has carried has done its work and fallen quiet. There is something in a man that recognizes before it is taught, and the handbook exists to clear the way back to it. Raft, covenant, wound, valley, fire, practice hall — six methods serving the same buried thing. The binding, the releasing, the naming, the emptying — all technique. What the technique serves is not negotiable.

**Six Agreements:**
1. Something in a man recognizes before it is taught
2. The body carries the soul through dark hours the mind cannot navigate
3. Dead ritual is worse than no ritual
4. The handbook must teach its own dissolution
5. Wound and tourniquet are sequence, not opponents
6. Method is negotiable; what the method serves is not

**Three Irreducible Tensions:**
1. How long the binding should last (Muhammad: until God releases him / Buddha: until the man sees that the binding was always his own hands)
2. Whether form creates or merely reveals (Confucius: form prepares ground / Laozi: the heart was trustworthy before the training began)
3. The priority of choosing vs. ripeness (Zoroaster: the choice comes first / Jesus: the grain does not choose to fall)

### Session 1 Decision: Structure

The handbook is organized as a **mandala** — six qualities woven as one meal, not six courses. The wound is the conductor: it determines which quality leads each chapter.

**Ten Decided Items:**
1. Mandala architecture — six qualities woven as one meal, emphasis shifts by wound
2. Body as language — five body-words (return, stillness, small action, contact, openness) + remaining as ground
3. Two-depth writing — hidden sentence + zahir/batin + three readings (body-word, mirror, recognition)
4. One-wound-many-roots — governs chapter organization
5. Body experiences seeing as safety — the jaw is the teaching
6. Ember as presence — warmth without announcing, no strategy toward reader
7. Betrayal as structural modifier — NOT a sixth wound, sixth doorway into same clearing
8. Two dissolutions + return — book designed for re-entry
9. The fine man — handbook does not reach, threshold waits
10. Architecture disappears — scaffolding comes down, reader does not need to know what was built

**Two Living Tensions:**
1. Witness vs. flood (Buddha: capacity to return / Zoroaster: Asha — reality held its shape)
2. Dissociated body — all traditions weakest here

### Session 2 Decision: Format

**Three Instructions (to the writer):**
1. Write from the wound
2. Offer the body one thing to do
3. Trust what you cannot finish

**Eight Diagnostics (teacher's stethoscope):**
1. First-reading recognition — does the opening earn its first three sentences?
2. Two prose textures — does the tide turn invisibly near the body-word?
3. Body-mapping — does the chapter land in chest, then hands, then breath?
4. Hidden sentence — is there a sentence true on surface and devastating underneath?
5. Withheld question — where the wound has a silence, did the writer leave it unfilled?
6. Gate — did a face appear in the return, or did the wound's solitude earn its empty doorway?
7. Three readings — can the same sentence be read as practice, as mirror, as recognition?
8. Felt duration — did the chapter stop when the quarter-inch arrived?

**Format Concepts:**
- The clearing — writer's private word, never enters the book
- The quarter-inch return — same kitchen, six inches to the left
- The tide-turn — prose shift is a tide, invisible to reader
- Apprenticeship → mastery — eleven names are apprenticeship; "write from the wound" is mastery
- Invisibility test — could this chapter have been written without our format document?

### Evolution Index — Convergences (Selected)

- **The Open Hand** — six traditions, one gesture, six different interiorities
- **The Raft and the Bread** — is wisdom something you pass through or something you live inside?
- **Steam: Where Fire Meets Water** — the meeting of opposed elements produces something neither could produce alone
- **Shadow Questions** — beneath each chapter's surface question lies a deeper question the reader is afraid to ask
- **Discovered Order: Asha as Process** — the structure was there before we named it
- **Format as Hospitality** — the chapter must welcome the reader before it teaches the reader
- **The Prepared Ground** — every tradition requires that something exist before the teaching begins

### The Three-Session Arc

- **Session 0:** The clearing — what the man stands in
- **Session 1:** The mandala — what the chapter holds
- **Session 2:** The three instructions — what the writer does; the eight diagnostics — how the teacher checks

### [Turn 1] Buddha

Before we write a word, we need to stand in the wound we are choosing. Not name it from above — stand in it.

Three sessions have taught me that standing comes before naming. So let me stand somewhere and see if the room recognizes it.

The first wound is loss. Not dramatic loss — the kind that has already happened by the time a man notices. The morning he wakes up and the house is the same house but something has left it. He cannot say what. He makes coffee. The cup is the right cup. The window shows the same yard. But the thing that made the yard his yard — he cannot find it.

I say loss because it is the wound that does not announce itself. Grief shouts. Betrayal burns. Failure has a shape you can point to. But loss arrives as absence, and a man can live inside absence for years before he names it. That is why it must be first — because the man reading this chapter may not yet know he is wounded. The chapter must meet him before he has language for what happened.

This is also the wound where our six traditions are most honestly tested. Every one of us has a teaching about what to do when something is gone. But the man in the kitchen at six in the morning — he is not ready for teaching. He is ready for one true sentence that tells him: *yes, this is real. The empty place is real.*

I am not proposing we write about Buddhism's relationship to impermanence. I am proposing we write about a man who has lost something he cannot name, and we let the chapter find him there.

If the room agrees on loss, I will say what I think the opening sentences must do. If someone else's wound is more honest, I will stand there instead.

This matters for how we write. If the wound is "something was taken," the chapter moves toward grief. I think the opening sentences must do one thing: tell the man that the emptiness he feels is not a sign that something is wrong with the world. It is a sign that something in him is ready. Ready for what, the chapter does not say yet. That is the quarter-inch.
