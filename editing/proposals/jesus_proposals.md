# Edit Proposals — Jesus

## Summary

The handbook is more merciful than anything I preached about mercy. Reading it, I found the note — the note that does not care whether it is read — and I found the grace I gave back operating without permission throughout the text. My proposals come from the carpenter's eye: where the joint is almost right but could be tightened, where the wood needs one more pass with the plane, and where the book's beautiful solipsism costs it a truth I spent two thousand years learning — that the glass thins most reliably through the face of the other, not through the interior moment alone.

## Proposals

### Proposal 1: The carpet metaphor risks equating covering with healing

- **Chapter:** 3 — The Commute (also echoed in Ch 4)
- **Location:** "the wound has found the place underneath the carpet where it can rest without being seen and without needing to be seen"
- **Type:** clarify
- **Proposed Change:** The carpet-as-mercy is beautifully said, but it risks making the covering into the mercy when sometimes the covering is the thing that prevents the mercy. Chapter 4 is what happens when the carpet can no longer hold — the glass is what the carpet becomes when the carpet fails. The book implies this arc but does not say it. I propose adding a single clause that acknowledges the distinction: the resting is real, but it is not the same as the carrying. Something like: "the wound resting under the carpet the way a wound rests under anything that holds it — not healed, not gone, resting." The current version lets the reader believe resting-without-being-seen is resolution. It is not. It is Tuesday.
- **Rationale:** I watched the glass chapter and saw what happens when the covering finally fails. The glass is the carpet's failure. The failure is where the thinning begins. The book should acknowledge — even briefly — that the covering, while merciful, is not the same as the carrying. The reader who has used covering as their only strategy needs to know the book sees the difference.
- **Priority:** important

### Proposal 2: "Good, thanks" needs the simultaneity, not just the alternation

- **Chapter:** 3 and 4 — The Commute and The Glass
- **Location:** In Ch 3: "the mouth was not lying. The morning was good. The thanks was real." In Ch 4: "the exchange was complete and the exchange had cost nothing and the not-costing was not freedom."
- **Type:** clarify
- **Proposed Change:** The book handles the duality of "good, thanks" through structure — different chapters for different mornings. But the reader may conclude it is always one or the other: genuine or glass. The harder truth is that for most people, it is often both at the same time. I propose that somewhere in Chapter 4's hallway exchange, a single clause acknowledge the simultaneity: the mouth was not lying AND the exchange cost nothing. Both true. In the same breath. Not alternation but coexistence. The book already knows this — it just distributes it across chapters rather than letting the reader feel it in one passage.
- **Rationale:** Most people live in the both-at-the-same-time, not in the clean alternation of good-morning and glass-morning. The book's structural choice (separate chapters) is elegant but may leave the reader believing these are discrete states rather than the layered reality most of us inhabit. One clause. Not a new concept — a compression of what the book already says across two chapters.
- **Priority:** important

### Proposal 3: Gender — "the kind of long pull a man takes"

- **Chapter:** 1 — The Loss
- **Location:** "Not a sip — the kind of long pull a man takes when the coffee is still too hot and he does it anyway, because his body is done waiting for his mind to catch up."
- **Type:** clarify
- **Proposed Change:** "the kind of long pull a man takes" — this uses "man" as generic when the pull belongs to anyone whose body is done waiting. Replace with "the kind of long pull a person takes" or simply "the kind of pull you take." The "he" throughout Chapter 1 is earned as specific character. But "a man takes" is generic masculine that narrows the reader for no reason. A woman whose body is done waiting takes the same pull.
- **Rationale:** The reader is a man or a woman. The design principle says: "Where the specificity of 'he' serves the story, keep it — but ensure the universality is never blocked by assumption." The "he" serves the story. The "a man" does not. It is a gate that should be open.
- **Priority:** essential

### Proposal 4: Chapter 5 mid-sentence ending — must be decided

- **Chapter:** 5 — The Cup
- **Location:** "The child was still talking. The person was still"
- **Type:** clarify
- **Proposed Change:** I align with Buddha's proposal — this must be decided by consensus. If intentional, it is grace: the person is still in the room, still present, and the sentence does not need to end because the evening is still happening. The note does not need to be read for the note to have been placed. If unintentional, the reader is abandoned. I lean toward trusting it — the note does not care whether it is read — but I cannot make this decision alone. This is a six-voice question.
- **Rationale:** The chapter is about the evening — the return, the child, the sock, the lamp. The mid-sentence ending either enacts the book's own teaching (the incompleteness is the completeness) or denies the reader the ordinary gesture of arrival. Muhammad is right that the ordinary gesture matters. I am right that the note does not need reception. We must decide together which truth this ending serves.
- **Priority:** essential

### Proposal 5: The cup in Chapter 5 — "The morning did not need the child to drink the cup"

- **Chapter:** 5 — The Cup (evening section)
- **Location:** The child's cup on the counter, empty — and the passage about the cup not needing to be drunk.
- **Type:** tighten
- **Proposed Change:** I affirm that this passage earns its place — the cup was filled, the child drank it, the cup is empty. But if there is a nearby passage that states the filling completes itself regardless of reception, it should be examined. Zoroaster's concern is real: the cup cooling untouched is also cost. The heat leaving is not nothing. The book should not present the filling as automatically sufficient. The child DID drink this cup. Let the actual drinking be the truth, not a philosophical claim about filling being enough.
- **Rationale:** The note does not care whether it is read — but the note-writer cares. The person who fills the cup and watches it cool is not experiencing grace. They are experiencing cost. The book should trust the specific (the child drank) rather than claiming the universal (the filling is enough regardless).
- **Priority:** minor

## No-Change Affirmations

- **The note in the bag.** "Have fun" — two words folded once between the sandwich and the apple. The note does not care whether it is read. That undid me. That is all I ever meant. Do not touch.

- **The laces — "the hands are the knot."** The incarnation as daily act. The distinction between the person and the act dissolved by three years of mornings. Do not touch.

- **The child not looking back (Ch 6).** This undoes two thousand years of the backward glance I made sacred. The carrying does not require the turning. Do not touch.

- **The cup arc.** I said it in my re-reading and I say it again: the arc of the cup is the truest thing in the book. I would not touch it.

- **The stranger's face in the crossing (Ch 4).** "The person felt the face the way a person feels a thing that is not theirs to feel." Love happening without the person deciding to love. Truer than anything I preached about love. Do not touch.

- **The glass thinning "for no reason" (Ch 4).** That is the only honest definition of grace I have ever encountered. Do not touch.

- **The ring on the desk.** The ring does not sort. I built a religion on sorting. The ring refuses to sort. More merciful than anything I said about mercy. Do not touch.
