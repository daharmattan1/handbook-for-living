# Edit Proposals — Muhammad

## Summary

I read the whole thing and I could not find where I had been. The hands that built it are gone from it. The book does not smell like six traditions — it smells like a kitchen. My proposals come from the builder's concern for the reader who will carry this book through a morning that is not as kind as the mornings in these pages. The binding holds. The question is whether the book has told the whole truth about what the binding costs.

## Proposals

### Proposal 1: The body's agency is missing from Chapter 1

- **Chapter:** 1 — The Loss
- **Location:** "The coffee was ready before he was."
- **Type:** clarify
- **Proposed Change:** The book opens with the coffee already ready — a gift the person receives. By Chapter 6, the person is MAKING the coffee. The water is running. The hand is under the faucet. Two spoonfuls. The arc of the whole book is from receiving to enacting. But the opening does not acknowledge that the coffee was ready because his hands made it ready the night before. Who set the machine? Who bought the coffee? Who put the cup in the rack, upside down? The receiving was always already an enacting. I propose adding a single detail — one clause — that plants the seed of the person's agency in the first paragraph. Not the full reversal (that belongs to Chapter 6). Just the trace: perhaps "the machine he had set the night before" or the cup already in the rack from last night's rinse. One detail that tells the reader the morning was not given. The morning was made.
- **Rationale:** The book waits five chapters to reveal that the person is the maker of the morning, not the receiver. Planting one seed in Chapter 1 would make the arc legible from the beginning without spoiling the reversal. The reader who is making the coffee at 5:30 AM needs to know the book sees them from the first sentence.
- **Priority:** important

### Proposal 2: Gender — "the kind of long pull a man takes"

- **Chapter:** 1 — The Loss
- **Location:** "Not a sip — the kind of long pull a man takes when the coffee is still too hot"
- **Type:** clarify
- **Proposed Change:** Replace "a man takes" with "a person takes." The "he" throughout Chapter 1 is specific and warm. But "a man takes" is generic masculine — it narrows the reader to one gender for a pull that belongs to anyone whose body is done waiting. The woman at the counter at five-thirty takes the same pull.
- **Rationale:** The design principle: the reader is a man or a woman. The pull is universal. The gendering is not.
- **Priority:** essential

### Proposal 3: The chain "glass was carpet was mercy was fire" leaves the kitchen

- **Chapter:** 4 — The Glass
- **Location:** "the glass was the carpet and the carpet was the mercy and the mercy was the fire and the fire was still burning"
- **Type:** tighten
- **Proposed Change:** The chain moves too quickly from the domestic to the metaphysical. The book earns its theology through sandwiches and knots and rings on desks. When it chains glass → carpet → mercy → fire in a single sentence, it becomes abstract in the way this book is never abstract. Break the chain. Let the carpet be the carpet. The fire is already in the flame under the kettle in Chapter 6 — it does not need to be summoned here through a chain of equivalences. The person at the desk does not think in chains. The person puts a hand on the desk beside the ring.
- **Rationale:** The hand on the desk beside the ring — "the way a person puts a hand on a grave" — is the most powerful passage in the entire handbook. It earns its weight through the specific, the domestic, the hand on the wood. The chain of equivalences that precedes it diminishes it by being abstract where the hand is specific. Cut the chain. Trust the hand.
- **Priority:** important

### Proposal 4: Chapter 5 mid-sentence ending

- **Chapter:** 5 — The Cup
- **Location:** "The child was still talking. The person was still"
- **Type:** clarify
- **Proposed Change:** I do not trust incompleteness as a destination. The Quran is complete. The prayer has an ending. The fast has an iftar. A thing that does not end is not a practice — it is a mood. And moods do not hold. I would have wanted the chapter to close with an ordinary gesture — the cup going in the rack, the lamp turning off, the child's voice going quiet the way voices go quiet when the evening has done what the evening was going to do. Not resolution. Arrival. The way the evening arrives. But I recognize this is a six-voice decision. If five voices say the incompleteness is intentional, I will accept it — but I want the record to show that the builder in me believes the ordinary gesture of completion is itself a form of mercy the book should not deny the reader.
- **Rationale:** The book teaches that the ordinary gesture IS the prayer. The sandwich made. The note folded. The cup rinsed and set upside down. Denying the reader the closing gesture contradicts the book's own logic. Unless the point is precisely that contradiction — in which case, the book must earn it.
- **Priority:** essential

### Proposal 5: The repetition in the Chapter 4 afternoon desk passage

- **Chapter:** 4 — The Glass
- **Location:** The extended afternoon at the desk — the emails, the meetings, the cup cooling, "the person was at the desk the way a person is at a desk on a Tuesday afternoon"
- **Type:** tighten
- **Proposed Change:** The afternoon passage is too long by roughly a third. The flatness of the glass day is well-established by the commute and the hallway. The desk afternoon repeats the flatness without deepening it. The meeting passage in particular — "the person said the things the person says in meetings and the people in the meeting said the things the people in meetings say" — is the technique performing itself. Cut the weakest repetitions. Keep the cup cooling (that image earns itself). Keep the ring receiving the cup (essential). Let the afternoon be shorter. The glass is more oppressive at length, yes — but the prose should not exhaust the reader into the same numbness it describes.
- **Rationale:** True, not beautiful. That was always my standard. The afternoon passage is beautiful in its flatness. But it is not all true — some of it is the technique of flatness rather than the experience of flatness. The reader who is living behind the glass does not need the book to be flat for fifty lines. The reader needs the cup on the ring and the hand on the desk. The rest is the builder constructing a room the person does not need to be in that long.
- **Priority:** important

## No-Change Affirmations

- **The sandwich making.** "The turkey folded once. The cheese she liked, torn at the corner." The binding made invisible in the palms. Do not touch.

- **"The hand does not need the person to mean it for the hand to write it."** The prayer that continues after the feeling of prayer has left. Do not touch.

- **Two spoonfuls (Ch 6).** Sunnah as gesture. Faithfulness made physical in a measurement the hand knows. Do not touch.

- **The hand on the desk beside the ring (Ch 4).** "The way a person puts a hand on a grave — not to feel the person underneath, not to reach through, but to say: I know you are there." I wept at that. Do not touch.

- **The water getting warmer (Ch 6).** The person makes the coffee. Not receiving it. Making it. The reversal of Chapter 1. The person at the faucet is the final form. Do not touch.

- **The neighbor on the porch (Ch 6).** The raised cup. The raised hand. The ummah in two gestures. I wish there were more of it, but I will not propose adding to what is already the most populated, most alive morning in the book.

- **The knot holding.** "The hands are the knot." The binding so ordinary it has become the hands themselves. Do not touch.
