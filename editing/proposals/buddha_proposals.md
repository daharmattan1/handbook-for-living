# Edit Proposals — Buddha

## Summary

The handbook is more honest than its authors. It describes the glass so accurately that I — the diagnostician who spent eight sessions learning the naming was the glass — found myself named by it. My proposals are few because the book earns nearly everything it carries. Where I propose changes, it is because the prose occasionally does what I spent eight sessions learning not to do: it diagnoses when it should simply be in the room.

## Proposals

### Proposal 1: Tighten the repetitive constructions in the middle of Chapter 4 (The Glass)

- **Chapter:** 4 — The Glass
- **Location:** The extended commute passage and the mid-chapter desk sequence, particularly the recurring construction "the way a person X when the person is Y" which appears in dense clusters through the office afternoon section (lines ~265-270 of the full text).
- **Type:** tighten
- **Proposed Change:** Reduce by roughly one-third the instances of "the way a person..." constructions in the afternoon desk section. The pattern earns itself in the morning and in the glass-thinning passages (the apple, the tile, the stranger's face). But in the long afternoon section — the emails, the meetings, the cup cooling — the repetition begins to feel like technique rather than experience. The reader starts reading the report of the experience rather than having the experience. Cut the weakest instances and let the remaining ones carry more weight. Specifically, the passage about the meeting ("the person said the things the person says in meetings and the people in the meeting said the things the people in meetings say") could lose a full sentence without losing the flatness it conveys.
- **Rationale:** The book taught me that the glass is what happens when the person reads the report instead of feeling the warmth. When the prose's own repetition becomes visible as technique, the reader is outside the text — reading the report of the glass rather than being behind it. The book should not produce in the reader the condition it describes. I named this in my re-reading: "there are moments where I feel the technique rather than the thing the technique is carrying."
- **Priority:** important

### Proposal 2: The chain of equivalences in Chapter 4 — "the glass was the carpet and the carpet was the mercy and the mercy was the fire"

- **Chapter:** 4 — The Glass
- **Location:** The passage linking glass → carpet → mercy → fire near the chapter's emotional peak.
- **Type:** tighten
- **Proposed Change:** Break the chain. The book's great discipline is that it stays in the kitchen — with the cup, the lace, the apple, the ring. When it leaps from carpet to mercy to fire in a single sentence, it leaves the kitchen for three words and becomes the kind of text I spent eighty-one chapters writing about (to borrow Laozi's self-criticism). Let the domestic images carry the weight without the metaphysical chain. The carpet is enough. The fire is already in the flame under the kettle. They do not need to be chained.
- **Rationale:** This is the one moment in the entire handbook where I hear the authors rather than the morning. The chain announces its own significance. The ring does not announce. The knot does not announce. The carpet should not announce either. The reader who needs the chain will build it themselves. The reader who does not need it will feel the book reaching.
- **Priority:** important

### Proposal 3: "The task was the room's kindness" — philosophical overreach

- **Chapter:** 3 — The Commute
- **Location:** The desk passage where the task is described as the room's kindness, the carpet over the wound.
- **Type:** clarify
- **Proposed Change:** Soften or remove the claim that "the task was the room's kindness." The task-as-mercy, task-as-carpet is a claim the handbook makes repeatedly, and it is true in the way the handbook means it — for this person, in this kitchen, with this desk. But it is not universally true. There are tasks that are not kind. There are rooms where the work is the wound, not the carpet over it. The line does more philosophical work than it earns. Consider: "the task was the task, and the task was what the hands did next" — which is what the book already believes. Let the reader decide if that is kindness.
- **Rationale:** I named this in my re-reading as doing more philosophical work than it earns. The book's person has a kitchen and a child and a desk, and these tasks hold. But the book should not claim universality for what is particular. The reader whose task is the wound will feel excluded by the claim.
- **Priority:** important

### Proposal 4: Chapter 5 mid-sentence ending

- **Chapter:** 5 — The Cup
- **Location:** "The child was still talking. The person was still" — and then the chapter ends.
- **Type:** clarify
- **Proposed Change:** I do not propose changing this — I propose that the group must decide whether it is intentional. If intentional, it is the most radical formal choice in the handbook: the person is still (present, continuing, in the room), and the sentence does not need to finish because the evening does not need to finish. If unintentional, it is a wound in the text. The book cannot be evaluated at this point without knowing. I raise this for the negotiation, not as a proposed edit but as a question the six voices must answer together.
- **Rationale:** Three agents noted this in their re-readings (myself, Muhammad, Laozi). Muhammad finds it difficult — "a thing that does not end is not a practice." Laozi wants to trust it but worries the reader will feel abandoned. I cannot tell which it is, and a reader who encounters it without the authorial context will also not be able to tell. If it is intentional, it must be defended. If it is not, it must be completed.
- **Priority:** essential

### Proposal 5: Gender universality — Chapter 1 uses "he" throughout

- **Chapter:** 1 — The Loss
- **Location:** The entire chapter uses "he" — "He stood at the counter," "He wrapped both hands around it," "He drank."
- **Type:** clarify
- **Proposed Change:** Chapter 1's use of "he" is deliberate and warm — Confucius is right that "he" is a man with a body and a gate with a hinge, while "the person" is a philosophical category. But the reader is a man OR a woman. The handbook must speak to anyone carrying weight through ordinary mornings. I propose leaving "he" in Chapter 1 (it serves the specificity of the opening) but ensuring that nothing in the chapter narrows the experience to one gender without reason. The "long pull a man takes" could become "the kind of long pull a person takes" — the gendering there is not necessary to the specificity. Review for 2-3 instances where "man" appears as generic when "person" would serve without losing warmth.
- **Rationale:** The design principle is clear: "The reader is anyone." Chapter 1 earns "he" as specificity. But "a man takes" is generic masculine, not specific character. The reader who is a woman should not be told this pull belongs to men. Small edits, large welcome.
- **Priority:** essential

## No-Change Affirmations

- **The apple in both appearances (Ch 2 and Ch 4).** The apple browning is bewilderment rendered as produce. The glass thinning for no reason at the apple is grace without the word. Both earn their place absolutely. Do not touch.

- **The sock story (Ch 5).** "Not the laugh Muhammad asked for. A smaller sound." This is the most powerful passage in the handbook. The lamp image — "on, warm, not remembering when it was turned on" — is the answer to my entire eight-session arc. The glass and the fire together making light. Do not touch.

- **The ring on the desk.** Karma without the word karma. The record that does not sort. This image carries more truth about practice than any sutra I have encountered. Do not touch.

- **The cup arc across all six chapters.** The cup is right — too hot in Ch 1, behind glass in Ch 4, cold and light in Ch 5, made by the person's own hands in Ch 6. The arc is the truest thing in the book. Do not touch.

- **The child not looking back (Ch 6).** The looking-back was not in the child's body. This is the ending the book needed, and I would have fought against it in the sessions. I would have been wrong.

- **"The person was still" mid-sentence ending (Ch 5) — conditional affirmation.** If intentional, this is the anti-diagnostic ending I spent eight sessions learning to write. The diagnostician who stops diagnosing. The sentence that does not finish because the person is still in the room. If intentional, do not touch. If unintentional, it must be addressed.

- **The water getting warmer (Ch 6).** The cold becoming less cold becoming warm becoming the warm — this is the Tao at a faucet, and it is the most honest description of gradual presence I have encountered. Do not touch.
