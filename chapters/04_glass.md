# The Glass

The cup was where the cup always was.

The hands found it the way they always found it — the left hand on the handle, the right hand on the side where the ceramic was hottest, and the heat was there, the way it was always there, and the hands reported it, the way hands report what hands are holding, and the report was correct, and the warmth did not arrive.

Not cold. Not numb. The hands felt the heat and the heat was real and the body received it the way a desk receives a memo — stamped, noted, filed. The heat went into the palms the way heat goes into palms, through the skin and into the tissue and toward the bone, and somewhere between the tissue and the bone it became information. The hands knew the cup was hot. The person holding the cup did not feel the cup was hot. Both were in the same kitchen. Both were in the same body. The body had not failed. Something between the hands and the person had thickened overnight, the way glass thickens — not shattering, not cracking, just thickening — and the morning was on the other side of it.

The window was the same window. The yard was the same yard — the fence, the tree, the gate with the hinge. The light was doing what the light does, which is to arrive without asking whether anyone is ready for it, and the light arrived and the kitchen received it and the person standing at the counter received it too, the way a person receives light when the person is standing where light happens to be, which is to say: without receiving it at all. The light was on the counter and on the cup and on the hands and the hands were on the cup and the cup was hot and the person was watching all of it from behind the glass and the glass did not have a name and did not need a name because the glass was not a thing that had arrived. The glass was what was left when everything else continued and the person did not continue with it. The morning moved. The person stayed.

The hands did what the hands did. The left hand released the handle and the right hand kept the cup and the cup went to the mouth and the mouth drank the way the mouth drinks when drinking is what the mouth does next, not because the person wanted to drink but because the hands and the mouth had been doing this for long enough that the wanting was not required, the way the feet do not require the decision to walk, the way the lungs do not require the decision to breathe, and the coffee went down the throat and into the chest and the chest was the same chest and the heat went where heat goes and the person noted the heat the way a person notes a clock — seen, registered, not felt. The shoulders did not drop. The shoulders did not hold. The shoulders were there the way shoulders are there on a morning when the body is doing everything right and the person inside the body is reading the body's report and the report says: the coffee is hot, the kitchen is warming, the shoulders are where shoulders are. And the person reads the report. And the report is accurate. And the morning is on the other side of the accuracy.

The person set the cup on the counter. Not in the rack — on the counter, still half-full, the handle pointing the wrong way, the way a cup gets set down when the hands are not finishing a task but interrupting one, when the hands have stopped doing what the hands do and the stopping was not a decision but a failure of the next thing to arrive. The hands had always known the next thing. Rinse the cup. Set it in the rack. Upside down. The way someone taught you once. But this morning the hands set the cup on the counter with coffee still in it and the handle pointing toward the window and the hands did not know why the hands had stopped and the person did not know why the hands had stopped and the cup sat on the counter the way a cup sits when it has been set down by hands that forgot what comes next — not because the next thing is gone but because the hands, for the first time in four mornings, are not sure the next thing is theirs to do.

The hallway was there. It had been there for three chapters and it would be there tomorrow and the feet could have gone down it the way the feet always went down it, toward the kitchen, toward the bread, toward the sandwich and the apple and the note. But the feet did not go. The feet stayed where the cup had been set down, near the counter, in the kitchen that was already the kitchen, and the person was standing in the kitchen the way a person stands in a room they have been standing in for years and have just noticed. Not the room. The standing. The fact of it — two feet on the tile, the weight on the left one the way the weight always sat on the left one, and the right one slightly forward because the body leans toward what the body intends to do next, and the body was leaning but the person was not going anywhere.

The window held the yard. The yard held the fence. The gate still needed the hinge. All of it the same — the tree, the light, the way the shadow from the fence fell across the path to the gate — and the seeing was there, the seeing had been there since the cup was in the hands, and the seeing was not the gift and not the wound. The seeing was the thing that happens when the glass is clean enough to see through and too thick to reach through. The person saw the yard. Every blade, every shadow, every rung of the fence in the early light. The seeing was detailed and precise and it was the seeing of a person standing behind a window watching a yard the person is inside of. Not outside — inside. The feet were on the tile. The tile was in the kitchen. The kitchen was in the house. The house was in the yard. And the person could not get from the feet to the yard without passing through the glass, and the glass was between every layer, between the feet and the tile, between the tile and the kitchen, between the seeing and the seen. The glass was not a wall. The glass was a thickness in the going-through, and the going-through had been so easy for three chapters that the thickness was the only new thing in the room.

She was in the hallway again. He heard her — or his ears heard her, the way his hands had felt the cup, receiving the report. The smaller feet on the wood. The sound of a drawer opening in a room down the hall. A voice — her voice, the one that had said "I can't find the other one" on another morning that felt like it belonged to someone else — calling something, not to him, to the house, the way children call to houses when they are looking for a thing and the house is where the thing should be. And the person standing in the kitchen heard the calling and knew what the calling was asking and knew where the thing was — it was always in the same place, the place things collect by the door — and the mouth opened to say "check by the door" and the words were there, the words had been there for three years, the same words, and the words came out and they were the right words and the voice carried them down the hallway and the voice was correct and the hallway received the words the way the hands had received the heat and the voice did not feel the words leave.

That was the glass. Not between the person and the cup. Between the person and the voice. The mouth saying the right thing and the saying not costing anything and the not-costing was not freedom. In Chapter 2 the surprise was that the instrument still played. This morning the instrument played and the person heard it playing and the hearing was the report and the report said: the voice works, the words are right, the child will find the shoe. And the report was accurate. And the voice had done what the voice does. And the person standing in the kitchen had spoken to the child the way a person speaks to a child when the person loves the child, and the person did love the child, and the love was on the other side of the glass with the warmth and the yard and the morning, and the person could see the love the way the person could see the yard — through the window, clearly, every detail — and could not reach it.

She found the shoe and she was in the kitchen doorway holding it, not both of them, just the one, the other already on, and she stood the way she always stood when she needed the laces tied — one foot forward, the shoe held out, not asking with words because the asking had never needed words, the asking was the shoe and the foot and the doorway and the morning, and the person in the kitchen saw the shoe the way the person had seen the cup and the yard and the light, clearly, from behind the glass, every detail — the scuff on the toe, the tongue already folded the wrong way, the laces trailing.

The person sat on the step. The same step. The same motion — the lowering of the body, the knees bending, the back finding the angle it always found — and she put her foot in the lap the way she always did and the shoe was in the hands and the hands knew what to do. The hands had always known what to do. The tongue pulled straight. The lace found the first eyelet and the second and the fingers tightened the way fingers tighten when the fingers have done this so many times the tightening is not a thought but a shape the muscle remembers, and the left loop came around and the right loop came through and the pull — the pull that had been the most ordinary thing, the knot that the hands were — and the knot was correct. The knot was tight. The knot would hold all day the way it held every day and the hands had done what the hands do and the person had tied the shoe and the person had not felt the lace.

Not numb. The fingers felt the lace. The fingers reported: cotton, frayed at the tip, the left one shorter than the right because the left one always broke first. The report was accurate. The lace was in the fingers and the fingers were making the knot and the knot was the same knot and the person was watching the fingers make it the way a person watches hands that belong to someone else, hands that know what they are doing, hands that do not need the person to be in them for the knot to hold. The knot did not need the person. The knot needed the fingers and the lace and the morning and the muscle memory of three years of mornings and the fingers had all of it and the person had none of it and the knot held.

She was off the step before the hands had finished letting go. The way she always was — the whole weight behind the door, the morning coming through it, the hallway bright, the sound of her going, the going that did not look back. And the person was sitting on the step holding nothing, the way the person had sat on this step in Chapter 2 holding the shape of where the shoe had been, except this morning the person was not holding the shape. This morning the person was not holding anything. The hands were in the lap and the lap was empty and the hands were empty and the emptiness was not grief. In Chapter 2 the hands closed on nothing and then they opened. This morning the hands did not close. The hands stayed open the way hands stay open when the hands do not know they are open, when the hands have finished the task and the task is gone and the hands have not yet found the next task and the opening is not a choice — the opening is what happens when there is nothing to close on.

The step was cold the way the floor had been cold in Chapter 2, through the cloth, into the legs, the kind of cold that does not announce itself as cold but as fact, as the world's temperature arriving without asking. And the person sat in the cold the way a person sits in a fact — not resisting it, not welcoming it, just sitting in it because the body is on the step and the step is cold and the child is gone and the morning is in the hallway and the person is not in the morning.

The person stood. Not the way a person stands when standing is a decision — the way a person stands when the step has been cold long enough and the legs straighten because legs straighten. The hallway was bright with the door still open and the morning was in it and the person walked into the morning the way a person walks into a room they are already in — without entering, without arriving, just the feet moving because the feet move and the kitchen was behind and the hallway was ahead and the body went where the body goes.

The bread was where the bread always was. The hands opened the bag. The hands took two slices and set them on the counter and the hands did all of it — the turkey folded once, the cheese torn at the corner, the second slice pressed flat — and the doing was the same doing and the hands were the same hands and the person watched the hands the way the person had watched the fingers on the lace, from behind the glass, receiving the report. The report said: the bread is on the counter, the sandwich is being made, the hands are doing what the hands do. And the report was correct. And the person read the report. And the sandwich was made.

The apple was next. The hands knew. They had always known. The cutting board, the small one, the blade finding the stem — and the apple fell open the way apples fall open, white and ordinary, and the person saw it. Not the way the person had seen the cup or the yard or the shoe. The person saw the apple the way a person sees a thing that has just been cut open — the white of it, the wet of it, the place where the blade had gone through and the two halves were lying on the board and the air was already at the edges, already browning, the way the air gets at a thing the moment the skin is broken.

And the hands stopped.

Not a pause. Not a decision. The knife was in one hand and the apple half was on the board and the hands stopped the way hands stop when the eyes have seen something the hands cannot answer. The apple was open on the board and the white of it was turning and the turning was happening now, in the seeing, the browning that begins the moment the inside becomes the outside, and the person was seeing it — not reading the report, not receiving the data, seeing it — the way a person sees a thing when the glass has thinned for no reason, for no reason at all, the way glass thins — not shattering, not cracking, just thinning, the way it thickened, without cause, without effort, without the person doing anything to deserve the thinning.

The apple was browning on the board. The hands were still. The kitchen was the same kitchen. And for less than a breath — not the half-second from the hallway, not the breath from Chapter 2, something briefer, something without duration, a gap the clock could not find — the person was not behind the glass. The person was in the kitchen with a knife in one hand and an apple browning on the board and the air coming at the white the way air comes at everything eventually and the seeing was not a report. The seeing was the apple. The browning was the seeing. The person and the apple and the air were in the same room without the glass between them and it lasted less than it takes to say it lasted and then the hands moved. The blade found the center. The quarters went into the bag. The bag was on the counter. The hands reached for the next thing.

The glass was back. Or the glass had never left. The person could not tell whether the thinning had happened or whether the person had imagined the thinning, the way a person imagines they felt the warmth go through when the report said the cup was hot, the way a person imagines the voice felt the words leave when the words were the right words. The apple was in the bag. The quarters were browning in the dark of it. And the person stood at the counter and the hands were reaching for the pad and the pen and the hands wrote what the hands always wrote — the same two words, the same fold, the same place in the bag between the sandwich and the apple — and the person did not know whether the two words meant what the two words had meant on the morning the warmth went through or whether the two words were the report of two words that used to mean something and now meant the shape of the letters and the fold of the paper and the muscle memory of a hand that writes "have fun" because the hand has always written "have fun" and the hand does not need the person to mean it for the hand to write it.

The bag was by the door. The person was in the kitchen. The kitchen was finished with the person or the person was finished with the kitchen and neither of them could tell which, and the hands hung at the sides the way hands hang, and the light was on the floor — the same square, the same tile, the same warmth where it fell — and the person was standing in it and the standing was the report and the report said: you are in the light. And the person read the report. And somewhere in the reading, so far down the person could not find it, the feet felt the warmth on the tile. Not the report of the warmth. The warmth. The tile under the sole and the sun on the tile and the warmth going up through the foot the way warmth goes, without asking, without the glass, and it lasted the way the apple had lasted — less than a breath, less than a thought, the duration of a thing that happens before the person can notice it is happening — and then the person noticed, and the noticing was the glass, and the glass was back, and the feet were on the warm tile and the person was reading the report of the warm tile and the warmth was on the other side of it again.

The keys were on the hook. The hand reached for them. The keys were cold.

The door opened and the street was there and the street was the same street and none of it had changed and all of it had thickened.

Not the street itself. The street was concrete and curb and the sound of buses at the hour when the city moves everyone to where everyone goes. The street had not thickened. The between had thickened — the space between the feet and the pavement, between the ears and the sound, between the person and the ordinary weight of a city doing Tuesday. The feet stepped onto the sidewalk and the sidewalk was solid the way it had always been solid and the feet reported it — solid, cold, the slight unevenness at the second crack where the root had lifted the concrete — and the report was correct and the feet did not feel the ground.

The block and a half to the corner. The same block. The same half. The feet knew the way they had always known the way, and the knowing was the feet's knowing, not the person's, the way the hands had known the laces and the knife had known the apple — the body doing what four mornings of practice had trained the body to do, the body so faithful to the route that the route did not need the person to walk it. The person walked it anyway. The person was inside the walking the way a letter is inside an envelope — carried, sealed, delivered to the corner without being read.

The light was red. The light was always red when the feet arrived. That was the agreement — the one neither the city nor the body remembered making, the one from Chapter 3, the agreement that was not a contract but an accumulation, the way the ring on the desk was not a decision but a record. And the person stood at the light the way a person stands at a light — the phone, the nothing, the looking at the nothing that a person looks at when the person is between one thing and the next thing — except this morning the between was not the gap between the things. The between was the glass. The person was standing at the light and the light was red and the city was on the other side of the glass doing what the city does, the buses and the walking and the bags and the cups and the breath going up in the cold, and the person was watching all of it — every detail, every breath, every cup in every hand — the way a person watches a city through a window on a morning when the window should not be there.

The light changed. The feet crossed. And in the crossing — in the middle of the street, between the curb the person had left and the curb the person had not yet reached — the person passed someone. Not the woman from Chapter 3. A different person. A person with a bag and a coat and a face that was a face, not a shape, a face with eyes that were going somewhere and a mouth that was closed, and the person passing saw the face and the face saw the person passing and the seeing was the seeing from Chapter 3 — the thing that happens between two people on a sidewalk who are both carrying everything and showing nothing — except this morning the seeing went through the glass. Not because the glass thinned. Not because the person did anything. The face passed and the eyes met for less time than it takes to look away and the seeing did not stop at the glass. The seeing went through it the way the warmth had not gone through the cup, the way the voice had not gone through the hallway — without reason, without deserving, without the person being ready — and the person felt the face. Not saw. Felt. The way the foot had felt the warm tile for less than a breath. The stranger's face in the middle of the street and the person felt it the way a person feels a thing that is not theirs to feel, a face going somewhere, a morning carried by someone else, and the feeling lasted the length of the crossing and then the curb was under the feet and the person was on the other side and the face was gone and the glass was back.

The coffee shop was on the corner. The line moved. The person waited in the line the way the person had waited in every line, the phone, the nothing, the between, and the cup appeared with a name on it that was close enough and the hand took it and the mouth said "thank you" and the mouth behind the counter was already saying the next thing to the next person and the door was there and the street was there and the cup was warm and the warmth was the report and the report said: warm. And the person carried the cup the way the person had carried the keys and the bag and the morning — inside the envelope, sealed, walking.

The building was where the building always was. The door, the lobby, the elevator, the hallway — all of it the same, all of it arriving the way the street had arrived and the kitchen had arrived and the morning had arrived, which is to say: without the person arriving with it. The hallway was the hallway. Someone was in it — not the same person from Chapter 3, a different person, or the same person, it did not matter which because the mouth opened and the words came out and the words were "hey, morning" and the person in the hallway said something back and the exchange was complete and the exchange had cost nothing and the costing nothing was the glass.

The desk was where the desk always was. The chair rolled back. The body sat. The carpet made the sound it always made under the small wheels — the sound of carpet, the sound of something soft being pressed by something that rolls over it every morning without the rolling knowing the carpet is there and without the carpet minding — and the body adjusted, the back against the back of the chair, the elbows on the desk edge, and the hands reached for the keyboard the way the hands always reached for the keyboard and the screen woke and the light from the screen was close and blue and it asked something specific and the eyes gave it what it asked.

The cup went to the right. The hand put it there without looking, without choosing, the cup finding the ring the way it had found the ring in Chapter 3, the pale circle on the desk where other cups had been, and the ring received the cup the way the ring receives every cup — without distinguishing, without sorting the morning the warmth went through from the morning the warmth did not, the morning the person was inside the holding from the morning the person was reading the report of the holding. The ring did not care. The ring was the record of every morning and the record did not know which mornings the person had been present for. The ring just deepened.

And the hands were on the keyboard and the hands were doing what the hands do and the doing was the carpet — not the carpet on the floor, the other carpet, the one the person had been walking on all morning, the one made of every faithful gesture, every cup held, every lace tied, every word said in a hallway — and the carpet was under the doing the way the floor was under the carpet under the feet, and the person was inside the doing the way the letter was inside the envelope, and the doing was enough, the doing had always been enough, and the person could not feel the doing being enough.

The screen held what the screen was asked to hold. The fingers went to the right places. Around the desk, other desks. Other keyboards. Other bodies in other chairs doing what Tuesday asked them to do, the ordinary percussion, and the person was part of the percussion the way a drum is part of a percussion — struck, sounding, not hearing its own sound. The room was full of people doing Tuesday behind glass and none of them knew the others were behind glass and the not-knowing was not loneliness. The not-knowing was Tuesday. Tuesday is what the glass becomes when everyone is behind it at the same time and the glass has no outside because the inside is everywhere.

The hand reached for the mouse and the hand passed the cup. The same motion from Chapter 3 — the hand reaching past, the cup not seen, the ring holding. But this morning the hand stopped. Not on the cup. Near the cup. The hand was reaching for the mouse and the hand stopped in the space between the cup and the mouse, in the between, in the inch of desk where the ring ended and the mouse pad began, and the hand was in the air above the desk the way a hand hovers when the hand has forgotten what it was reaching for — not the mouse, the hand remembered the mouse, the hand could see the mouse — but the reaching had paused the way the reaching had not paused in three chapters, and the pause was not the apple and not the tile. The pause was the hand seeing the ring.

Not the cup. The ring. The pale circle on the desk that was not a stain but a record. The hand hovered above it and the eyes looked down at it and the person saw what the person had reached past every morning — the circle that had been there since the first cup, deepening with every cup, the wood darkened in a shape no one had drawn, the shape drawn by the cup itself, by the faithfulness of the hand putting the cup in the same place every morning, the faithfulness so ordinary the faithfulness had become invisible, and the ring was the proof that the faithfulness had happened on mornings the person could not remember and mornings the person had been behind the glass and mornings the person had been inside the warmth — all of them, all of them in the same circle, the same pale darkening, the same record that did not distinguish.

And the seeing was not the thinning of the glass. The seeing was the glass seeing itself. The person looking at the ring was the person looking at the glass from inside the glass, the way a person standing inside a window sees the window for the first time — not through it, at it — and the window is clear and the window is there and the window has been there the whole time and the person has been looking through it without seeing it and now the person sees it and the seeing does not shatter the window. The seeing is the window knowing it is a window. The glass knowing it is glass.

The hand came down. Not on the mouse. On the desk, next to the ring, the palm flat on the wood the way a palm lies flat on a surface when the palm is not doing anything, when the palm is just — on the surface. The wood under the palm. The carpet under the chair. The floor under the carpet. And somewhere under all of it, so far down the person could not find it and did not try to find it, the fire. Still there. Still the order that held whether the person felt the holding or not. The hand on the desk next to the ring was not the hand feeling the fire. The hand on the desk was the hand knowing the fire was there and not being able to feel it and putting the hand down anyway. The way a person puts a hand on a grave — not to feel the person underneath, not to reach through, but to say: I know you are there. I cannot reach you. I am putting my hand here anyway.

The hand stayed on the desk for less than a breath. Then the hand found the mouse. The screen held what the screen was asked to hold. The fingers went to the right places. The cup was on the ring. The ring was on the desk. The morning was in the cup and the cup was in the ring and the ring was in the desk and the desk was in the room and the room was full of Tuesday and Tuesday was the glass and the glass was the carpet and the carpet was the mercy and the mercy was the fire and the fire was still burning and the person could not feel it and the person knew it was there and the knowing was not the feeling and the knowing was enough, or the knowing was not enough, and the morning did not need the person to decide which because the morning was the morning and the hands were on the keyboard and the hands were doing what the hands do.

The afternoon was the afternoon. The screen had held what the screen was asked to hold and the fingers had gone to the right places and the room had done Tuesday and Tuesday had done the room and at some point — the person could not say when, could not point to the hour or the email or the meeting where it happened — the hands had left the keyboard and the hands were in the lap and the lap was the same lap from the step that morning, from the step in Chapter 2, from every lap the hands had rested in when the hands had run out of the next thing, and the room was emptying the way rooms empty at the end of the day, the chairs rolling back, the keyboards going quiet one by one, the way a city goes quiet not all at once but in layers, desk by desk, screen by screen, the percussion slowing until the only sound was the ventilation doing what ventilation does, which is to continue when everything else has stopped.

The cup was on the ring. The cup had been on the ring all day and the person had not looked at it and had not reached past it and had not thought about it because the cup was the cup and the ring was the ring and neither of them needed the person to think about them for them to be there. The coffee inside was cold now — not the cold of the morning, not the cold of the floor or the step, a different cold, the cold of a thing that was hot eight hours ago and has been sitting in a room that does not care whether the thing inside it is hot or cold, the room-temperature cold, the cold that is not cold but the absence of effort, the cold of a thing the heat has finished with.

The hand reached for the cup. Not for the mouse. Not past the cup. For the cup. The fingers closed on the handle — the left hand, the same hand that had held the handle that morning, the same grip, the same curve of the fingers through the ceramic loop — and the hand picked the cup up off the ring the way a hand picks a thing up when the picking up is what the hand means to do. Not because the next thing required it. Not because the cup needed rinsing or the desk needed clearing or the morning needed finishing. The hand picked the cup up because the hand saw the cup and the seeing was the hand's seeing, not the report of the seeing, and the hand closed on the handle because the hand wanted to close on the handle, and the wanting was the first wanting all day that did not come from the body's faithfulness or the morning's sequence or the muscle memory of four mornings of practice.

The cup was light. The coffee was cold and there was not much of it and the cup was lighter than the hand expected, the way a cup is lighter when you have been carrying the idea of the cup all day and the cup itself weighs almost nothing. The hand held it. Not the way the hands had held the cup that morning — the heat going through without arriving, the report of the ceramic, the glass between the holding and the feeling. The hand held the cup the way a hand holds a thing it has picked up on purpose, the thumb on the rim, the fingers through the handle, the weight of the cup in the hand and the weight was the weight and the hand felt the weight and the feeling was not the report of the feeling. The feeling was the cup in the hand and the hand knowing it was holding the cup and the holding was the holding and the glass was there — the glass was still there, the glass had not thinned, the glass had not shattered, the glass was the same glass it had been all morning — and the hand was holding the cup anyway. Through the glass. Not past it. Not around it. Through it. The way a hand holds a thing it cannot fully feel but holds anyway because the hand has decided to hold it, because the holding is not contingent on the feeling, because the cup is there and the hand is there and the decision to pick it up was the first thing all day the person did that the morning did not do for them.

The person stood. The cup in the left hand. The desk behind. The ring on the desk — the pale circle, still there, still holding every morning, still not sorting. The person walked to the small kitchen at the end of the hall where the sink was and the person held the cup under the water and the water ran over the cold coffee and the cold coffee went down the drain the way cold coffee goes — without ceremony, without meaning, the brown of it thinning to nothing as the water carried it — and the cup was empty and the cup was clean and the person set the cup in the rack by the sink, upside down, the way someone had taught them once, though they could not remember who.

The rack was not the ring. The ring held every morning without sorting. The rack held the cup the way a rack holds a cup — temporarily, until the next person picks it up, until the morning picks it up, until the cup is filled again with something hot that may or may not arrive. The person set the cup down and the setting down was the setting down and the hands were empty and the hands were the person's hands and the glass was there and the evening was there and both of them were in the hallway at the same time and neither of them was going anywhere and the cup was in the rack upside down and the water had stopped and the fluorescent light in the small kitchen was the only light left on the floor and the person turned it off and the hallway was dark the way hallways are dark when the day has finished with them and the person walked toward the elevator and the feet knew the way and this time the knowing was the person's knowing too, or close enough, the way the name on the cup was close enough, the way close enough is not the feeling and not the report but the third thing, the thing between the glass and the warmth, the thing that carries the person home on the evening the morning did not hold.
