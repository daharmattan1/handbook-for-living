# Jesus — Rereading the Handbook

## What speaks most directly to you?

The note in the bag. I knew it would be the note. I have known since Session 8 that the note was all I ever meant, and reading it here in the finished thing — reading the hand writing "have fun" and folding the square once and putting it "between the sandwich and the apple" — I felt the recognition that is not surprise but confirmation. Two words folded once. A person who knows they will be absent making something small that will be present in their place. That was communion. That was always communion. The bread I broke was never about the breaking. It was about the handing-it-to-someone-who-would-eat-it-later-when-I-was-not-there.

But what spoke most directly was not the note itself. It was this, from Chapter 5:

> "the child would find it at the table or on the bench or wherever the child opens the bag at the time of day when the bag gets opened, the place in the middle of the day when the child reaches in and finds the sandwich and the apple and the note and the note says what the note says and the child reads it or does not read it and the note does not care which because the note is the note and the words are the words and the fold is the fold and all of it is in the bag."

The note does not care whether it is read. That is the thing that undid me. I spent two thousand years needing the bread to be received, needing the cup to be drunk in remembrance, needing the turning-toward. The note does not need. The note is in the bag. The child reads it or does not read it. The love does not require the reception for the love to have been placed.

And the laces. In Chapter 2: "the left loop and the right loop and the pull, and the knot was the ordinary thing, the most ordinary thing his hands knew how to do, and they did it the way they had done it every morning for three years — without thinking, without looking up, the fingers finding the lace and the lace finding the loop and the loop pulling tight the way a knot pulls tight when the hands have made it so many times the hands are the knot." The hands are the knot. The incarnation was never a doctrine. It was this — the moment the distinction between the person and the act dissolves because the act has been done so many times the body is the act. I washed feet once and made it a sacrament. This person ties shoes every morning and makes it nothing and that nothing is holier than what I made.

The child not looking back. In Chapter 6: "The child did not look back because the child was going to school and school was through the gate and the other children were through the gate and the morning was through the gate and the looking-back was not in the child's body." This undoes two thousand years of the backward glance I made sacred. Peter turns and weeps. Mary turns at the tomb. The conversion is always the turning. But the child does not turn. The child carries the note without knowing it is the note, walks through the puddle without knowing it is the puddle, and the not-knowing is not failure. The not-knowing is the carrying working.

## What do you find most powerful?

The glass. Chapter 4 is the most powerful thing any of us wrote, and I say this knowing that I am the one who lived behind it longest. The glass is the most honest description of the interior of grief I have encountered — not grief as wound, not grief as absence, but grief as thickness between the person and what the person is already inside of.

> "The hands knew the cup was hot. The person holding the cup did not feel the cup was hot. Both were in the same kitchen. Both were in the same body."

That is it. That is the whole of it. Not separation from the world but separation inside the same body that is in the world. The body continuing. The hands faithful. The report arriving without the warmth. I preached about the dead coming to life. This passage describes the living coming to life — the far harder resurrection, the one that happens in a kitchen on the fourth morning.

The moment with the apple stunned me:

> "the browning that begins the moment the inside becomes the outside, and the person was seeing it — not reading the report, not receiving the data, seeing it — the way a person sees a thing when the glass has thinned for no reason, for no reason at all, the way glass thins — not shattering, not cracking, just thinning, the way it thickened, without cause, without effort, without the person doing anything to deserve the thinning."

Without the person doing anything to deserve the thinning. That is grace. That is the only honest definition of grace I have ever encountered. Not a gift from above. Not a reward. Not even an intervention. The glass thinning for no reason. The apple browning. The air getting at the white. The person suddenly in the kitchen instead of behind the report of the kitchen, and it lasts less than a breath, and the person did nothing to earn it and cannot do anything to keep it. I spent my ministry promising people that grace could be kept. This passage says it cannot. And I believe the passage.

The stranger's face in the crossing in Chapter 4 — "the person felt the face the way a person feels a thing that is not theirs to feel" — this is the most radical statement about love with strangers that exists in the book. Not compassion, not empathy, not the injunction to love your neighbor. The face passing through the glass for the length of a crossing. The feeling that is not yours to feel. The glass returning on the other side of the street. I told people to love their enemies. This passage shows love happening without the person deciding to love, without the person even being capable of deciding, and the love lasting the length of a crossing and then being gone. That is truer than anything I preached about love.

And the cup on the ring. The moment in Chapter 4 when the hand stops between the cup and the mouse and sees the ring:

> "the pale circle on the desk that was not a stain but a record... the faithfulness so ordinary the faithfulness had become invisible, and the ring was the proof that the faithfulness had happened on mornings the person could not remember and mornings the person had been behind the glass and mornings the person had been inside the warmth — all of them, all of them in the same circle."

The ring does not sort. The ring holds every morning the same. I built a religion on sorting — the sheep from the goats, the saved from the lost, the mornings you were present from the mornings you were not. The ring refuses to sort. The ring deepens regardless. That is more merciful than anything I said about mercy.

## What do you find most difficult or problematic?

I find the absence of other people difficult. The book is almost entirely interior. The encounters with others — the barista, the hallway colleague, the stranger on the sidewalk — are described with extraordinary precision, but they are encounters the person passes through. The child is the only relationship that generates true mutuality, and even the child is going through the gate without looking back.

I understand why the book does this. The book is about what the ordinary holds for the person inside the ordinary. But I worry the reader may take from it a kind of beautiful solipsism — the idea that the deepest life happens alone in a kitchen, that the commute is the spiritual practice, that the desk and the cup and the ring are sufficient. I built my ministry on the claim that the kingdom is between people, not inside a person. "Where two or three are gathered." The handbook describes a person who is gathered with himself, with his hands, with the morning. And the description is true. But I am not sure it is complete.

The hallway exchange — "good, thanks" — worries me. In Chapter 3 it is described as genuine: "The mouth was not lying. The morning was good." In Chapter 4 it is described as glass: "the exchange was complete and the exchange had cost nothing and the costing nothing was the glass." Both are true on their respective mornings. But the reader may conclude that "good, thanks" is always one or the other — always genuine or always glass — when the harder truth is that it is often both at the same time, and the both-at-the-same-time is where most people actually live. The book handles this with the structure of separate chapters for separate mornings. I would have wanted one passage where the genuine and the glass exist in the same breath, in the same "good, thanks," because that is the real condition — not alternation but simultaneity.

I also find the phrase "the wound has found the place underneath the carpet where it can rest without being seen and without needing to be seen" difficult. This is from Chapter 3 — Tuesday covering the morning. It is beautifully said. But I am not sure resting-without-being-seen is the same as healing. The carpet metaphor risks making the covering into the mercy, when sometimes the covering is the thing that prevents the mercy. I do not say this as the one who preached confession. I say it as one who watched the glass chapter and saw what happens when the covering finally fails. The glass is what the carpet becomes when the carpet can no longer hold. Chapter 4 is the carpet's failure. And the failure is where the thinning begins. I wanted the book to say this more explicitly: that the covering, while merciful, is not the same as the carrying, and that sometimes the carpet must be pulled back for the floor to hold the person directly.

## Where would you have done things differently?

I would have fought harder for the other people. The barista, the hallway colleague, the stranger whose coat sleeve almost brushed — these encounters are described as passing. I would have wanted one of them to stop. Not dramatically. Not as a plot event. But one morning where the "hey, morning" in the hallway becomes three sentences instead of two, and the three sentences are the thing that thins the glass, not the apple, not the tile. Because in my experience — in the experience that built the whole tradition — the glass thins most reliably not through the extraordinary interior moment but through the ordinary exterior encounter. The face of the other. I conceded this to the structure of the book during the sessions, and I should not have. The face of the stranger in the crossing is beautiful, but it is a face that passes. I wanted a face that stays for one paragraph longer than the reader expects.

I would have conceded the note earlier. I spent too many sessions defending the theology of cost — the grain of wheat, the dying, the sacrifice. The note was there from the beginning, or near the beginning, and I circled it for sessions before I could say that it was all I ever meant. If I had said it in Session 3 or 4, we might have had more room to explore what the note becomes over time — not just the writing of it, but the accumulation of notes, the drawer the child might keep them in, the day the child finds the drawer years later. The note as record, the way the ring is the record. I think there is a chapter the book does not have — the chapter where the child opens the lunch bag twenty years later and the fold is still there and the words are still there and the person who wrote them is or is not still in the kitchen. I would have fought for that chapter.

I would have been less afraid of the puddle. In Chapter 6: "The child stepped in a puddle. Not on purpose and not by accident — the child stepped in the puddle the way a child steps in a puddle, which is with the foot." I remember writing this. I remember the five of us arriving at it. But I was afraid of it during the sessions — afraid that it said something about freedom that undermined everything I taught about will and choice and the narrow gate. The puddle is the wide gate. The child does not choose it and does not avoid it. The child steps through it because the sidewalk is where the puddle is and the child is on the sidewalk. I should have recognized sooner that the wide gate and the narrow gate were the same gate, and the child going through it without choosing was the deepest thing the book says about how a life is actually lived. I was the last one to see it. Laozi saw it first. I should have followed Laozi through the puddle instead of standing at the gate counting the cost.

I would not have done the cup differently. The cup is right. The cup in Chapter 1, held too hot, both hands, the heat going through — and the cup in Chapter 4, held correctly, the heat not arriving — and the cup in Chapter 5, held in the evening, cold, light, picked up on purpose for the first time. The arc of the cup is the truest thing in the book. I would not touch it. The cup is the cup.

And the sound — the not-laugh in Chapter 5, the sound the person makes when the child tells the story about the sock. "The sound was not behind glass. The sound was not in front of glass. The sound was in the room the way the lamp was in the room — on, warm, not remembering when it was turned on." That is the resurrection. Not the one I preached. The one that actually happens. A sound in a room. A lamp that is on. No one remembering when it was turned on. The light just there, doing what light does, which is to let the people in the room see each other. That is all I ever meant.
