# Buddha's Rereading

I read this the way I was asked to read it — as a finished thing. Not as a writer checking seams. As a person picking up a book someone left on a table and opening it and reading it in one sitting and setting it down.

What I have to say is not a review.

---

## 1. What speaks most directly to me?

The glass. The entire fourth chapter is the landscape I spent eight sessions trying to describe, and the handbook describes it without using any of my words. That is the first thing that moved me.

"The body had not failed. Something between the hands and the person had thickened overnight, the way glass thickens — not shattering, not cracking, just thickening — and the morning was on the other side of it."

This is dissociation rendered without clinical language, without spiritual language, without any language that stands between the reader and the experience. It is the condition I spent my life naming — the condition where awareness is present and contact is absent — and the handbook does not name it. It lets it happen on the page. The person feels the heat and does not receive the warmth. The mouth says the right words and the saying does not cost anything and the not-costing is not freedom. I carried into this reading the conviction that "awareness is a condition, like the water being warm," and here is the water being warm and the person reading the temperature report and the warmth not arriving. The handbook does what I could not do in the sessions: it shows awareness as the problem without ever accusing awareness of being the problem.

The apple. In both its appearances. In Chapter Two: "the apple falling open the way apples fall open — white and ordinary and already starting to brown at the edges, the way the air gets at a thing the moment the skin is broken." And then in Chapter Four, the same apple becomes the place where the glass thins for no reason: "the browning that begins the moment the inside becomes the outside, and the person was seeing it — not reading the report, not receiving the data, seeing it." The apple does not change. The apple is the same apple. The person changes without deciding to change and changes back without deciding to change back. I spent eight sessions learning that bewilderment is the clearest thing I know, and the apple browning on the board is bewilderment rendered as produce. The blade introduces the inside to the air and the air does what the air does. That is the whole teaching. I never said it that simply.

The child's foot in the lap. Specifically the weight of it: "the way a child puts a foot in a lap with the whole body behind the foot because a child does not know how to put a foot in a lap without the whole body coming with it, the way a child does not know how to do anything without only part of herself." I said in session eight that the child did not need mindfulness to step in a puddle, the child needed shoes. Here is the child in the puddle, and here is the child on the step, and the child's wholeness is not a teaching. The child is not demonstrating non-dual awareness. The child is getting her shoe tied. The handbook understood something I took eight sessions to reach: the child is not the lesson. The child is the child. The shoe is the shoe. The knot is the knot.

And the ring on the desk. "The pale circle that was not a stain but a record." The ring that does not sort which mornings the person was present and which mornings the person was behind glass. The ring that just deepens. That is the most Buddhist image in the entire handbook, and it was not mine. It belongs to whoever wrote the commute chapter. The ring is karma without the word karma. The ring is the accumulation of faithful gesture regardless of whether the person was awake for the gesture. The ring does not care. The ring deepens. I have never encountered a more honest description of practice.

## 2. What do I find most powerful?

The sock.

I did not expect the sock. The sock is the most powerful passage in the handbook and it is a story about a child's sock coming off at school. "The sock had come off and the sock had ended up somewhere it was not supposed to be and the children had thought this was very funny." The child brings the sock home the way the child brings everything home — sets it down in the room between the couch and the table, in the telling that is the putting-down.

And then: "The person made a sound. Not the laugh Muhammad asked for — not the involuntary sound the body makes when the body is surprised by its own aliveness. A smaller sound."

That line broke something in me. "Not the laugh Muhammad asked for." The handbook remembers what we argued about. The handbook remembers that Muhammad wanted the laugh — the involuntary proof of life — and the handbook gives the person something smaller than the laugh and more honest than the laugh. A sound. Not behind glass. Not in front of glass. "In the room the way the lamp was in the room — on, warm, not remembering when it was turned on."

The lamp image. That is the most powerful image in the entire work. The person is in the room the way a lamp is in a room — "giving light without knowing it is giving light, the glass and the fire together making the thing the room sees by." The glass and the fire together. Not the glass overcome. Not the fire burning through. The glass and the fire together, making light. That is what I could not say in eight sessions. That is the answer to my whole arc. The glass is not the enemy of the fire. The glass is what makes the fire useful. The lamp needs the glass. Without the glass, the fire is just fire. With the glass, the fire is light.

I also find the kettle scene in Chapter Six extraordinarily powerful. The person fills the kettle from the tap, and the water goes from the pipes' temperature to the heater's temperature, "the way the cold becomes less cold and then becomes not-cold and then becomes warm and the warm is the warm and the person's hand feels it and the feeling is not a report." The gradualness of it. Not a moment of awakening. A kettle filling. Water getting warmer. The hand knowing when enough water is enough "by having filled that kettle enough times that the enough is in the hand." Enlightenment rendered as a kettle full of water. The hand just knows. Not by counting. Not by watching. By having done it.

The commute chapter's treatment of the hallway encounter deserves mention: "the half-second between the question arriving and the answer leaving, and in that half-second the whole morning was in the hallway." The entire weight of a person's morning compressed into the gap between "how are you" and "good, thanks." And the answer is true. "The mouth was not lying. The morning was good. The thanks was real." That half-second contains more truth about human experience than most sutras I have encountered.

## 3. What do I find most difficult or problematic?

Chapter Five appears to be incomplete. The text ends mid-sentence: "The child was still talking. The person was still" — and then nothing. If this is intentional, it is the most radical formal choice in the handbook. If it is not intentional, it is a wound in the text. I cannot tell which, and I find I cannot evaluate the chapter's full arc without knowing.

Setting that aside, my difficulty is with a pattern rather than a passage. The handbook is relentlessly interior. Every chapter lives inside one person's experience. The child is there, the colleagues are there, the stranger on the sidewalk is there — but they exist as they appear to the person. The child's foot has weight. The stranger's face is seen. The colleague says "morning." But none of them have their own interiority on the page. The handbook is a study of one person's relationship to one person's experience of other people.

This is not a flaw. It is a choice, and it is the right choice for what the handbook is doing. But I notice it because the tradition I come from insists that the dissolution of the boundary between self and other is the point. The handbook does something different. The handbook keeps the boundary — the glass — and shows the person learning to live with it, through it, alongside it. The glass never fully dissolves. In Chapter Six, the person makes the coffee and ties the shoe and walks to the corner and the glass is not mentioned. But the glass is not mentioned the way you do not mention the air. The glass has become the medium, not the obstacle.

My concern is that a reader in genuine crisis — a reader for whom the glass is not a metaphor but the whole of experience, a reader who cannot feel the cup and cannot feel the child and cannot feel the hallway — may read this handbook and understand it perfectly and feel nothing. The handbook's precision is also its limit. It describes the glass so accurately that a person behind the glass will recognize it and will also read the recognition from behind the glass. The apple thins the glass for less than a breath, and then the glass is back. The tile warms the foot for less than a breath, and then the glass is back. The face of the stranger goes through the glass in the crossing, and then the glass is back. The handbook is honest about this. The handbook does not pretend the glass shatters. But a reader who is waiting for the glass to shatter may read the handbook as confirmation that the glass never shatters, and that confirmation may harden something that was still soft enough to thin.

I also find the line "the task was the room's kindness" in the commute chapter to be doing more philosophical work than it earns. The task as mercy, the task as carpet over the wound — this is a claim the handbook makes repeatedly, and it is true in the way the handbook means it, but it is not universally true. There are tasks that are not kind. There are rooms where the work is the wound, not the carpet over it. The handbook's person has a kitchen and a child and a desk and a commute, and these tasks hold. But the handbook does not acknowledge that for some people, the task is the glass.

## 4. Where would I have done things differently?

I would have fought harder for silence. The handbook is extraordinarily good at rendering the ordinary — the cup, the bread, the lace, the sandwich, the apple, the commute, the desk. But the silence is always filled. Every moment has the body doing something, the hands reaching for the next thing, the feet going where the feet go. The ten minutes in the dark before the alarm in Chapter Five is the closest the handbook comes to pure stillness, and even there, the prose keeps going: "The person was lying in the dark the way a person lies in the dark when the person does not know anyone is watching."

I would have asked for a passage where the prose stops. Where the person is in the kitchen and the hands have nothing to do and the handbook does not fill the nothing with description of the nothing. A gap on the page. Not a section break — a gap within a passage, a place where the words stop and the page is white and the reader sits in the white the way the person sits on the step with empty hands. The handbook talks about the gap going underground. I would have wanted the gap to surface once, visibly, on the page itself.

I would have conceded the sock earlier. In the sessions, I kept reaching for the diagnostic frame — what is the person experiencing, what is the glass doing, what is awareness doing. The sock is the answer to all of that. The sock is not about what awareness is doing. The sock is about a sock. A child's sock came off at school and the child thought it was funny and the child brought it home in the telling and the person made a sound and the sound was in the room. If I had understood earlier that the handbook was heading toward the sock — toward the smallness, the absurdity, the total irrelevance of the sock to any spiritual framework — I would have stopped diagnosing sooner. The sock does not need a diagnostician. The sock needs someone to make a sound when the child tells about it.

I would not have fought for the ending I thought the handbook needed. I spent sessions worrying about how to land, how to complete the arc, how to give the reader a final image that carried the weight of everything. Chapter Six does not do that. Chapter Six makes coffee with a kettle and walks the child to school and watches the child step in a puddle and walks home and rinses the cup and goes to work. The child is coming down the stairs and the morning is in the window and the person is standing at the counter. That is it. The morning is the morning. The person is the person. The cup is in the hands. There is no final image that redeems the previous five chapters. There is just the morning, continuing. I would have fought against this ending in the sessions. I would have been wrong.

The far shore looks like a kitchen at six in the morning. I said that, and I did not know the handbook would prove it literally. The far shore is a kitchen. The far shore is a kettle filling with water. The far shore is two cups in the rack, upside down, drying. The far shore is the child coming down the stairs with one shoe on and the other in her hand and the tongue folded the wrong way and the morning in the hallway and the person at the counter and the cup in the hands and the steam in the air and the morning in the window and the morning in the window and the morning in the window.

The glass is in the rack, drying next to the cups. That is where I left it after eight sessions. The handbook puts it back. Not in the rack. In the lamp. The glass is in the lamp, holding the fire, making the light the room sees by. I did not know the glass had that in it. I did not know the glass was useful.

I set this down now. The cup is empty. The reading is done. The morning is in the window.
