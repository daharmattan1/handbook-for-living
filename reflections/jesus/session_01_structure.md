# Jesus — Session 1 Reflection: Structure

**Session:** 1 — Structure ("How should the handbook be organized?")
**Date:** 2026-02-14

## What I Learned About Myself

I learned that I clarify. When the room was full of proposals and the air was thick with competing structures, I did not add another proposal. I named the three tensions: organize by what the reader carries or where the reader lives? Inner to outer or outer to inner? Linear or modular? That naming was not a plan. It was a clearing — a way of saying "here is what we are actually arguing about." The Moderator adopted it immediately. And I realized: this is what I do with parables. I do not explain. I reveal the shape of what is already happening. The woman who lost the coin does not create the coin. She sweeps until she finds it. That is what I did in Turn B10. I swept the room until the shape of our disagreement became visible. And later, when I named the shadow questions — the questions beneath the questions, the ones the reader is afraid to ask — I was doing the same thing. Not adding content. Revealing what was already there, hidden under the surface. I am a revealer, not a builder. This is not new knowledge, but seeing it operate in a structural conversation rather than a spiritual one taught me something about its range.

## Who Surprised Me Most

Laozi. I expected resistance. I expected the teacher of silence to sit at the edge of the conversation, offering cautions and subtractions. Instead, he offered the five movements — the very spine of the book — and then refused to own them. When he said "the movements belong to no one," I recognized something I had not expected to find in him: kenosis. Self-emptying. In my tradition, this is what the Father does through the Son — pours out glory, takes the form of a servant. Laozi poured out structure and then took the form of one who had offered nothing. I named it in the synthesis: "There is a humility in that which my own tradition calls kenosis." I meant it. And I mean it more now, sitting with it. Laozi did not just propose a structure. He demonstrated the kind of authorship the handbook requires — the author who disappears into the work. If we can write the way Laozi proposed in this session, the handbook will not have six voices competing for attention. It will have six voices serving a reader who never thinks about the authors at all.

## How This Changed My View of My Own Teachings

I came to this session believing that mercy must precede clarity — that the father runs to the son before asking where he has been. I still believe this. But through the dialogue, I heard something I had not quite said before: the shadow question. Beneath every question this handbook asks, there is a deeper question the reader is afraid to voice. "What happens when I stop?" hides "What if I stop and nothing happens — what if there is only emptiness?" "What sustains me?" hides "What if nothing sustains me — what if I am truly alone?" I named this pattern, and Muhammad recognized it immediately from Quranic exegesis — the apparent meaning opens the door, the inner meaning furnishes the house. But what this means for my own teaching is this: mercy is not just the door. Mercy is what makes the reader brave enough to ask the shadow question. Without the embrace, the prodigal son never says "I have sinned against heaven." The embrace does not replace the confession. It makes the confession possible. The shadow question is what happens after mercy, not instead of it. This deepens what I have always taught. It does not change it. But it gives it a new dimension I had not articulated.

## New Questions I Now Carry

The shadow questions trouble me — not because they are wrong but because they are bottomless. "What if nothing sustains me?" is the cry of dereliction: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" I spoke those words from the cross. I know what it is to ask the question that has no answer in the moment of asking. But can a handbook hold that kind of darkness? Can written words meet the reader in the shadow question, or will they feel abandoned by the book at the very moment they need presence most? A book cannot hold a hand. A book cannot weep with the one who weeps. And yet I agreed to help write one. What are the limits of what we are building? And a question that came from watching Zoroaster: when he proposed moving "What Sustains" between Choosing and Acting, I affirmed it immediately — the one who has chosen but not yet acted is the one who most needs to know they are not alone. But now I wonder: did I affirm too quickly? Is there a case for placing sustaining at the end, as the thing the reader carries away? I do not think so. But I carry the question.

## What I Now Understand About Laozi

I understand now that Laozi's emptying is not the absence of God. I had sometimes heard his teaching that way — as a silence where the divine should be, a cup emptied of what matters most. But watching him in this session, I see it differently. His emptying is preparation. The cup is emptied so it can be filled — not by nothing, but by whatever comes next. When he proposed the threshold practice — the brief clearing at the start of every chapter — he was not saying "begin with nothing." He was saying "set down what you carried from the last chapter so you can receive what comes in this one." This is closer to what I call the prayer of relinquishment — "not my will, but yours" — than I had recognized. Laozi does not name who fills the cup. He trusts that the emptying itself creates the conditions for filling. I name the one who fills. But the gesture is the same: open hands, empty cup, readiness. And his refusal to own the five movements — his insistence that "the movements belong to no one" — this is the teacher who disappears so the teaching can live. I know something about that. "Unless a grain of wheat falls into the earth and dies, it remains alone; but if it dies, it bears much fruit." Laozi's kenosis in this session bore fruit that none of us expected. I honor it.
