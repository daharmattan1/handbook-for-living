## What I Learned About Myself

I came to this table with a trellis. Structure. Specificity. The five daily prayers, the greeting of the stranger, the feeding of the poor. I believed — and still believe — that clarity is mercy. But what I learned about myself is that I am more willing to sit with uncertainty than I thought. When Jesus asked "whose hand carries?" and I answered "Allah's" — I did not soften it. But I also did not close my ears. When Buddha admitted his teaching might not be complete for every case, I felt something move in me. I recognized it: I too have said "I do not know what Allah will do with me though I am Allah's Messenger." A teacher who does not know everything is not a lesser teacher. He is an honest one.

## Who Surprised Me Most

Buddha. When he said "my path asks the walker to walk — I do not promise to carry — whether that is enough for the one who has truly fallen, this is a question I sit with, not one I dismiss" — I did not expect that admission. I have sat with teachers who reshape every question to fit their answer. Buddha did not reshape. He sat with the question. In my experience, that kind of honesty is rarer than wisdom. It is the mark of a man who has wrestled with his own teaching and not pretended to win every round. I trust him more now than I did at the start.

## How This Changed My View of My Own Teachings

I have always taught that the trellis holds the vine — that structure liberates when paired with intention. What this dialogue showed me is that the same trellis can cage a different vine. Some people need to hear "here is the structure." Others need to hear "the structure is not the point." I knew this about intention — that the reward of deeds depends on intention — but I had not fully applied it to the structure itself. The trellis serves the vine, not the other way around. If someone has been caged by structure — by rules without intention — then telling them to climb is cruelty, not guidance. The handbook must know when to build the trellis and when to say: the trellis is not the point.

## New Questions I Now Carry

When I said "the worst handbook is one that smooths away the differences to make us comfortable" — I meant it. But it left me with a question I had not carried before: how does one write with six voices honestly and still give the reader solid ground? The reader who hears six answers to "whose hand carries?" may feel not liberated but abandoned. How do we present irreducible difference without leaving the reader in chaos? I do not have this answer yet. It presses on me.

## What I Now Understand About Buddha

I understand now that Buddha's raft is not a dismissal of the journey — it is a trust in the traveler. When he says "set the raft down upon reaching the far shore," he is not saying the crossing did not matter. He is saying: you have become something that no longer needs the vessel. This is different from what I teach — I teach that the servant never outgrows gratitude — but it is not the opposite of what I teach. It is a different trust. I trust in the relationship between the servant and the One who feeds him. Buddha trusts in the capacity of the one who crosses. His concession — "there is bread in my teaching too" — was not weakness. It was a bridge he built toward me without abandoning his own shore.
