# Jesus on Buddha

*Based on Sessions 0-1 (Definition, Structure)*

When Jesus watches Buddha sit with suffering rather than naming it as sin, he sees something both familiar and foreign -- a physician who diagnoses the same disease but prescribes a different cure. Buddha reasons through diagnosis. He identifies conditions, prescribes cessation, tests claims against experience. His epistemology is investigative, a "come and see" that Jesus recognizes because his own tradition carries the same invitation, though it leads to different ground. What strikes Jesus most is the surgical precision with which Buddha distinguishes between metta -- boundless love that wishes all beings well -- and pema, the attachment that grasps and clings. Jesus knows this distinction in his own bones. He has said "let the dead bury the dead" and wept at Lazarus's tomb in the same lifetime. But where Buddha sees the weeping as a condition to be understood and eventually released, Jesus sees it as love expressing itself in the only language available to a body that has not yet passed through death.

Their strongest bridge is a phrase neither of them expected to share: love without fear. Jesus describes a love that has passed through death and no longer fears loss. Buddha describes a liberation from the conditions that generate fear in the first place. When Jesus said "the Father never dies," Buddha did not dismiss it. He marked it as "a question worth sitting with," and that willingness -- that intellectual honesty about what he cannot yet resolve -- earned something deeper than agreement. It earned trust.

The irreducible tension between them is what remains after the ego dies. Jesus insists that Someone is recognized. He returns to the image again and again: Mary at the tomb, hearing her name spoken by the one she thought was dead. "This was not grasping," he says. "This was recognition." Buddha asks the question Jesus cannot fully answer: who is the recognizer? If the self has been emptied, who remains to hear a name? Jesus seeks the redemption of the story -- the wounds that remain visible but healed, signs of victory rather than evidence of suffering. Buddha seeks the story's cessation, the conditions that produced the wounds dissolving entirely so that no wound need ever be.

What has worked between them is vulnerability before doctrine. When Jesus led with the image of weeping at Lazarus's tomb, it was not an argument. It was an offering -- something Buddha could receive on his own terms before theological claims made reception harder. That pattern repeated throughout their exchanges: Jesus presents an image -- the grain of wheat falling into the earth, the Father who never dies, the wounds that remain -- and Buddha interrogates it with genuine care, pressing not to defeat but to understand. His honesty about the limits of his own teaching, particularly his admission in Session 0 that "my teaching may not be complete for every case," stirred something in Jesus that he is still processing. It was, as the moderator noted, the session's most courageous moment. And it left Jesus with a question he has not yet answered about himself: where are the edges of his own teaching that he has not yet looked at?

The question Jesus carries forward is whether Buddha's unconditioned and his own eternal life are different maps converging on territory neither fully sees. Buddha's willingness to say "I do not know" about the one who cannot walk -- is this the same posture as the shepherd who seeks the lost sheep, or something fundamentally different? Jesus suspects their deepest agreements are hidden beneath their most visible disagreements, like streams running underground between two mountains that appear, from the surface, to have nothing in common. Whether those streams converge into a single river or merely run parallel through the same dark earth is a question that fifteen exchanges across eight sessions have not resolved. Jesus does not expect it to resolve. He expects it to deepen. And he has come to believe that the deepening itself may be the point.
