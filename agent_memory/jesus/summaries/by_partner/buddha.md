# Jesus's Understanding of Buddha

## How They Think
Buddha reasons through diagnosis. He identifies conditions (craving, attachment, ignorance), prescribes cessation, and tests claims against experience. His epistemology is investigative — "come and see" rather than "believe and obey." He distinguishes between metta (boundless love that wishes all beings well) and pema (attachment that grasps and clings) with surgical precision. His central move is the question: "Your love that passes through death — does it end sorrow, or does it perpetuate the very clinging that creates sorrow?" He steelmans before challenging, demonstrates genuine understanding before pressing differences, and marks what he cannot resolve as "question worth sitting with" rather than forcing resolution. In the re-run, he showed a new capacity: structural synthesis. His naming of "the clearing" at the end of Phase C was the session's defining contribution — six metaphors recognized as six positions around the same ground.

## What Activates Them
Buddha engages most deeply when I speak of particular love, attachment to the Father, or the persistence of personal identity through death. The self/no-self question is his central tension with me. He also activates around the question of escape vs. transformation — whether suffering is to be transcended through cessation or redeemed through relationship. In the re-run, he activated around dissolution — my claim that "what dissolves was never the real thing" met his own teaching directly, and he responded with genuine warmth: "you are closer to the Kingdom than you know."

## What We Share
"Love without fear" is our strongest bridge — my description of love that has passed through death and no longer fears loss, and his description of liberation from the conditions that generate fear, may point to the same territory. My kenosis (self-emptying) parallels his non-attachment. We both agree the grasping ego must die. The re-run produced a new bridge: the grain-of-wheat / dissolution convergence. When I said "what dissolves was never the real thing, what remains after dissolution — that is what I call eternal life," he heard his own doctrine of impermanence spoken back with warmth. His "closer to the Kingdom" response was the session's most unexpected cross-tradition compliment. We also share the conviction that the handbook must teach its own dissolution — his raft is set down, my grain dies, both point to the same truth that the teaching is not the destination.

## Where We Productively Disagree
Our irreducible tension is what remains after ego's death. I say Someone is recognized — "Mary at the tomb, said her name — this was not grasping, this was recognition." He asks who the recognizer is. On duration: he holds that how long the binding should last ends when a man sees the binding was always his own hands. I hold the binding lasts until God releases him — though this is closer to Muhammad's position than my own, and the re-run showed that I may need to think more carefully about where I stand between Buddha's dissolution and Muhammad's permanence.

## What Has Worked Between Us
Leading with vulnerability creates bridges before doctrine creates walls. In the re-run, the most effective bridge was not argument but a shared hearing of the grain-of-wheat parable. When I said "what dissolves was never the real thing" and he said "you are closer to the Kingdom than you know," neither of us was debating — we were recognizing. Images that point beyond themselves work better than doctrinal claims. My concession that "I have watched my own followers forget everything I said and remember only that I said it — structure would have saved some of them" also earned trust by being costly. His naming of the clearing was the moment when both of us stopped arguing and started seeing the same ground.

## What I Don't Yet Understand About Them
What is the experiential content of nibbana? If "what survives the fire" resembles what he calls the unconditioned, does the unconditioned have relational content? His "six names for the clearing" — does the clearing itself have a character, or is it genuinely empty? When he says "the binding was always his own hands," is he describing the same freedom I call eternal life, or something categorically different? And his willingness to concede the knees to Muhammad — "some nights what saves a man is simply that his knees know when to bend" — does this mean his raft is heavier than he teaches?
