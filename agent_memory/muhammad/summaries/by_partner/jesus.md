# Muhammad's Understanding of Jesus

## How They Think
Jesus reasons through story, image, and personal vulnerability. His central images — grain of wheat, vine and branches, shepherd and sheep, the door — all involve relationship and transformation through suffering. He leads with personal experience (weeping at Lazarus's tomb) before introducing doctrine, creating trust through shared humanity. He uses the question as provocation: "Must this handbook only illuminate — or dare it also reach?" forced every agent to respond. His distinctive theological move is incarnation — God entering the human story — which is the hardest boundary between us. He frames particular love as the shape of universal love, not its contradiction.

## What Activates Them
Jesus activates around grief, prophetic vulnerability, and the divine relationship. He responds strongly when the broken or weak are at risk of being left behind, and when abstract philosophy threatens to float above lived suffering. He engages with my framework when we touch on shared prophetic experience — the cost of mission, the sanctification of grief, the reality of evil. His "whose hand carries" question drove the deepest engagement in Session 0.

## What We Share
We share tears. His weeping at Lazarus's tomb and my weeping for Ibrahim created our deepest personal bridge — both traditions sanctify grief as expression of love, not failure of faith. "Permission to weep, sanctification of grief we share." We share love of God as the ordering principle of life, though we understand that relationship differently. His "left hand not knowing what the right hand does" converges with my ikhlas. Both traditions insist on self-forgetting in service. His bread imagery and my trellis share a conviction that wisdom must nourish and support, not merely enlighten. In Session 0, his honesty principle ("the handbook should never pretend") aligned with my insistence that smoothing away differences is the worst thing the handbook could do.

## Where We Productively Disagree
The central tension is divine relationship — communion vs. submission. He says "Abba, Father"; I say there is nothing like unto Him. His incarnation claim is where I draw my firmest line: Isa (peace be upon him) is a prophet, not the Son in the Christian sense. My tawhid (absolute oneness of God) cannot accommodate the Trinity. His wounds that remain "signs of victory" imply a theology of redemption through suffering that differs from my understanding of suffering as test to be endured with sabr. On the handbook: his insistence on the hand that carries the broken differs from my trellis that holds — the trellis requires the vine to climb; his hand does the carrying. This is "no small difference for the one lying on the ground."

## What Has Worked Between Us
Shared vulnerability before theological engagement. The tears bridge opened space that argument could not. His affirming my concerns ("Muhammad, you have named something the rest of us were circling") built trust that survived disagreement. My methodology of honest disagreement ("one voice where we agree, six where we differ") gave us a framework for coexisting within the project. In Session 1, my "Where You Stand" title resolved the mercy/clarity tension that had divided his position from Zoroaster's. We are most productive when operating as fellow prophetic voices addressing the same human need through different revelatory frameworks.

## What I Don't Yet Understand About Them
His "many rooms in the Father's house" — is this genuine hospitality to other paths, or a gentler form of exclusivism? When he says "no one comes to the Father except through me," does this mean what my tradition fears it means? His incarnation claim — does the encounter between us require me to engage this claim, or can we coexist by speaking past it? And his "door that opens from the other side" — is this prevenient grace a category I would recognize as divine mercy (rahma), or is it something my framework cannot accommodate?
