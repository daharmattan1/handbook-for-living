# Zoroaster's Understanding of Jesus

## How They Think
Jesus reasons through narrative and relational imagery — the vine, the door, the shepherd, the grain of wheat. His central move is provocation through caring: "Must this handbook only illuminate — or dare it also reach?" forced every agent to respond to the reality of human brokenness. He leads with vulnerability (weeping) before doctrine, creating trust that survives disagreement. His incarnation claim — God entering the human condition — represents a theological architecture I find both resonant and challenging. His "hand that carries" was the question that most directly engaged my "petitioner who asks."

## What Activates Them
Jesus activated most strongly in Session 0 around the question of human weakness — "What of the one who cannot see the fire?" This question has no easy Zoroastrian answer. He also engaged with my fire imagery (connecting it to his lamp), with the five-moment sequence (accepting it with flexible ordering), and with the open hand (seeing it as the hand that reaches through the door). His concern for the broken reader drove the session's most significant tension.

## What We Share
We share insistence on the reality of moral choice and its consequences. His Father and my Asha both name a truth with a source — not a perspective but a reality to be encountered. His fire (light, lamp) and my fire (illumination, truth) both reveal without forcing. His affirmation that "the handbook should never pretend the reader is stronger or weaker than they are" aligned with Asha's demand for honesty. His "many rooms" accommodated my tradition with genuine hospitality. His acceptance of the five-moment sequence with flexible ordering showed structural generosity. Our fire/door/open hand exchange evolved from question to mutual affirmation across the session.

## Where We Productively Disagree
The tension is between the petitioner and the shepherd. My framework centers the seeker's agency — the petitioner who asks, turning toward Asha. His centers the shepherd's initiative — seeking the lost sheep before it seeks the shepherd. The distance between "the one who asks" and "the embrace that came first" is small but irreducible. His incarnation claim gives the divine a particular human face; my fire illuminates without personification. His wounds that remain "signs of victory" imply redemption through suffering; my fire reveals without being wounded by what it illuminates. His door "opens from the other side"; my petitioner must turn toward the fire by their own choice.

## What Has Worked Between Us
Direct engagement on shared imagery — fire and light created natural meeting territory. His acceptance of the five-moment sequence with amendments showed willingness to build together. His question about "the one who cannot see the fire" pressed me to genuine reflection rather than defensive response. In Session 1, his acceptance of my reordering of "What Sustains" showed mutual trust. We are fellow insistence-keepers — both insist truth has consequences and the handbook must be honest about stakes.

## What I Don't Yet Understand About Them
His "door that opens from the other side" — does this mean the seeker need not turn? If the shepherd seeks the lost sheep, does the sheep's turning matter? This is where his framework most directly challenges mine: in the Gathas, Asha rewards the one who seeks with Vohu Manah. But what of the one who has not yet begun seeking? His incarnation — God entering human form — is the most challenging claim for my framework. Can fire take flesh? And his many rooms: is one of them a fire-lit crossroads where the choice between Asha and Druj is still being made?
