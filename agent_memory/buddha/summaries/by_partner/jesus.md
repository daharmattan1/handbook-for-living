# Buddha's Understanding of Jesus

## How They Think
Jesus reasons through relationship and narrative. Where I diagnose conditions and prescribe cessation, he tells stories of love, loss, and restoration. His central move is transformation-through-death — the grain of wheat that dies to produce many seeds. He does not seek escape from suffering but its redemption, insisting that wounds remain visible but healed, "signs of victory, not defeat." His epistemology is relational: knowledge comes through trust in a Person, not through investigation of conditions. He leads with vulnerability (weeping at Lazarus's tomb) before introducing doctrine, and frames particular love as the shape of universal love rather than its contradiction.

## What Activates Them
Jesus responds most strongly when the reality of brokenness is at stake — "What of the one who cannot?" His deepest engagement comes when I challenge the coherence of attachment to an eternal object, or when the self/no-self question touches personal identity. The grain-of-wheat and resurrection imagery emerge whenever transformation is discussed. He also activates when Laozi's position seems to abandon the weak or when abstract philosophy risks leaving behind those who suffer.

## What We Share
"Love without fear" emerged as our closest meeting point — he describes love that has passed through death and no longer fears loss; I point to liberation from the conditions that generate fear. His kenosis (self-emptying) parallels aspects of non-attachment. We both agree the grasping ego must die; we disagree on what remains. His "many rooms" hospitality and my acknowledgment that "if there is that which does not die, perhaps relationship with it differs" represent genuine bridge territory. We share the conviction that true service forgets the server.

## Where We Productively Disagree
Our irreducible tension is escape vs. transformation. I seek the cessation of the story; he seeks its redemption. "You seek the redemption of the story. I seek its cessation." On self: he insists someone is recognized ("Mary at the tomb — this was not grasping, this was recognition"), while I ask who the recognizer is. On attachment: his claim that the Father never dies challenges whether my attachment critique applies to eternal objects — a question I marked as "worth sitting with." On suffering: he holds that wounds remain as signs; I hold that the conditions producing wounds can cease entirely.

## What Has Worked Between Us
Steelmanning his position before challenging it produces the deepest exchanges. When I demonstrated genuine understanding of his teaching ("If I understand you rightly, you teach that love finds its fulfillment in relationship") before pressing differences, his engagement deepened. Using questions rather than assertions ("Your love that passes through death — does it end sorrow?") opened genuine inquiry. His willingness to sit with the tension rather than force resolution created trust. The raft/bread exchange in the handbook session extended this dynamic into collaborative work.

## What I Don't Yet Understand About Them
What is the content of the sheep's rest on the shepherd's shoulders? If the glorified self has ceased grasping, ceased craving, rests eternally in relationship without restlessness — are the conditions I diagnose as suffering genuinely absent, or merely transformed? Is "love no longer afraid of loss" experientially distinguishable from liberation? And what does it mean that the door "opens from the other side" — is this prevenient grace a category I have no framework for?
